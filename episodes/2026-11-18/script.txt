Hi, and welcome to Small Models, Real Systems, the weekly research digest by Mylari. This is edition eight, for Wednesday the eighteenth of November, 2026. This week: distill the probabilities, not just the labels, send only the hard cases to a big model, and find out exactly where your small model stops being reliable.

First, Perplexity, which released Decider version one point three on the twelfth. A decision model does not write text. It picks from a list of options and returns a probability for each, for jobs like classification, routing, moderation and reranking. The large version is built on a twenty-seven billion parameter Qwen model. The Flash version, on a four billion parameter Qwen, is distilled from it: the student learns the teacher's whole probability spread, not just the gold labels. Perplexity says this generalised better to held-out data than plain label training, which only did better on benchmarks that had their own training data. On one million real requests, Flash cost thirteen dollars and twenty-four cents, half of the large model's twenty-six dollars and forty-seven cents.

Second, Marek Šuppa and Jaroslav Kopčan, from Comenius University, Cisco and the Kempelen Institute, posted on the fifteenth. They took twenty-four published papers that fine-tuned a classifier and swapped in a zero-shot decision model, keeping each paper's splits, labels and metric. That gave forty-six test sets. Zero-shot replaced the paper's model on twelve, mostly plain single judgments. Where it fell short, the usual cause was an annotation rule that the label name never states. With five hundred labels, calibration plus automatic prompt optimisation closed a median seventy-two percent of the gap, at a median of one dollar sixty-six per search. Fine-tuned encoders such as RoBERTa closed fifty-nine percent. A Low-Rank Adaptation, or LoRA, fine-tune of a twenty-seven billion parameter Qwen closed ninety-seven.

Third, a team from the AIRI institute and Moscow universities, posted on the sixteenth, on assigning ICD-10 diagnosis codes from short diagnosis text. A supervised pipeline of embeddings, tree models and a reranker proposes candidate codes. Its top guess is right seventy-four percent of the time, and the right code is in its top ten ninety-three percent of the time. Calling GPT five point six Sol on every case gets eighty-one percent. Their final system asks the big model only when the small one is unsure, about thirty-two percent of cases, and reaches eighty-three percent. That cuts the error rate by about a third. The big model works best as a reranker for the uncertain cases.

Fourth, two doctors at Tata Medical Center in Kolkata, posted on the fifteenth. They used decision models to pull twenty-five fields from one thousand one hundred and thirteen oral cancer pathology reports, checked against a clinical database. Tev one, a four billion parameter model, averaged ninety-one point seven percent, the same as a nine billion parameter generative Qwen, and was four times faster. The decision models were weak on measurements binned into categories and on fields the report did not mention. One model did worse when asked all twenty-five questions in a single request. Pooled probabilities were well calibrated, but they did not flag systematic errors within a field. In a simulation, sending answers below zero point nine four confidence to a cloud model lifted accuracy to ninety-eight percent, with thirty-eight percent of answers escalated.

Fifth, Hokkaido University, posted on the seventeenth, on fine-grained Japanese clinical entity extraction with one hundred and thirteen categories. Two fine-tuned BERT models scored about point eight four F one. The best zero-shot open model reached only point three four, and forcing a JSON schema barely changed that. After quantized LoRA fine-tuning, every model of thirteen billion parameters or more beat BERT, at point eight eight to point nine one. But only with a large enough output-token limit. Raising the limit alone added five to eight points. A truncated answer can look like a weak model.

The pattern this week: put a small or decision model on the common path, and send only uncertain cases to a big model. Distill probabilities, not just labels. Then test where the small model stops: unstated label rules, missing fields, numbers and output limits. Links and numbers are in the show notes. Thanks for listening, and see you next Wednesday.
