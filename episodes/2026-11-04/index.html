Edition #6. Window: 28 October to 4 November 2026. Every number below comes from the linked source.

This week's theme: read the task before you pick the model, check that your thresholds travel, and judge a second opinion on what it actually fixes. A laptop benchmark shows when a fine-tuned encoder beats a prompted LLM and when it only ties; XDecideBench finds that English model picks and English-fitted confidence thresholds don't hold across languages; BERT students distilled from an LLM beat their teacher on the sentences that carry sentiment, while a constant "neutral" wins the headline metric; a study of selective escalation shows that uncertainty finds errors but other models rarely fix them; and a 560M Tajik embedder is fine-tuned in under an hour. Five items made the cut.

1. Do You Need an LLM for That? Classifying Text Locally

Damien Riquet (Lead Research Engineer, Hornetsecurity, a Proofpoint company) · blog, 1 Nov 2026 · driquet.info · code

What he did. A weekend benchmark on one Apple M5 laptop (24 GB) comparing four ways to classify text: TF-IDF plus logistic regression, a fine-tuned Ettin-150M encoder, Qwen3.5-4B prompted zero-shot and few-shot (BF16, MLX, reading label probabilities), and OneJev1.6-4B, a decision model built on Qwen. Three tasks chosen to differ in kind: CLINC150 intents (words give the answer away), VAST stance (the answer is in the relation between comment and topic) and DynaSent round 2 (sentences written by crowdworkers to fool sentiment models).

Evaluation. Official train and test splits, macro-F1, labels used, training time, median latency per text at batch size 1, peak RAM while predicting. Learning curves average three draws up to 100 per class.

Key results. - Full CLINC150 plus out-of-scope: TF-IDF 87.8 F1 (16 s training, 0.37 ms, 0.5 GB); Ettin 93.2 (16 min, 7.6 ms, 1.5 GB); Qwen zero-shot 79.7 (1.1 s, 17.6 GB); few-shot 79.0 (1.7 s); OneJev 95.7 (1.3 s, 17.7 GB). - More intents hurt the generic prompt, not the trained model: from 50 intents the prompt falls behind because more intents share the same words. - VAST: TF-IDF is at chance; Ettin 75.8; OneJev 80.6 but ten times slower; zero-shot far behind the encoder. - DynaSent: four of the five approaches are within 2.7 points (a tie on a 720-sentence test set). Zero-shot Qwen with a two-line prompt does as well as an encoder trained on all 13,065 sentences. - Labels needed for the encoder to beat both prompts: 25 per intent on CLINC, 333 per class (about 1,000) on VAST, never on DynaSent.

Caveats. One seed, one prompt, no tuning. The datasets are public and Qwen has likely seen them; OneJev's training data includes 206 CLINC150 training examples. One 4B LLM stands in for "an LLM".

Why it matters. The right answer depends on whether the words give the label away. If they do, a small fine-tuned encoder wins on quality, latency and memory with very few labels. If the words lie, labels buy little and a prompt is a fair choice.

2. XDecideBench: Evaluating Typed Decision Models and Confidence Policies Across Languages

Marek Šuppa (Comenius University in Bratislava; Cisco Systems), Jaroslav Kopčan (Kempelen Institute of Intelligent Technologies) · arXiv, 1 Nov 2026 · arXiv:2611.01370

What they did. A multilingual benchmark for Typed Decision Models, which return a probability for each declared option of a typed question (the kind used for routing, moderation and labeling). 9 suites, 122 languages, 88,425 texts and 153,875 typed questions, with a common scoring contract for valid answers, refusals and resampling.

Method. Scores eleven Typed Decision Models from ten developers, the Qwen backbones of three open heads, and a supervised mmBERT-base comparator fine-tuned on MASSIVE, SIB-200 and English MultiNLI. Tests confidence policies fitted on English (5% error threshold, conformal sets) unchanged on the other Core12 languages.

Key results. - The best model in English loses significantly in 56 of 232 suite-language cells. On Burmese Belebele, Brier skill is .336 for Microsoft's Decision-1 against .814 for Jev. - Three open fine-tuned heads beat their Qwen backbones in all 12 development languages, with larger gains in low-web-share languages, but they remove a similar share of backbone errors in both groups, so the larger gain may reflect weaker base models. - An English-fitted 5% error threshold lets through 7.8 to 12.4% errors on German and French XNLI. Jev admits 10.8% of Swahili decisions against 46.8% of English ones. - On the three datasets it was trained for, the supervised mmBERT comparator reaches .673 Brier skill; Laya, a decision model on the same encoder, reaches .274. - RTP-LX: the local gold toxicity label differs from the English one for 54% (4,016 of 7,400) of pairs. - Rankings move: GPT-6 Luna is 8th of 10 on Core12 but 1st on the native INCLUDE exams (.600 vs .536). Kendall correlation with the Core12 ranking is .33 to .64 on other suites.

Caveats. Hosted and local readouts can differ; several models could only be scored through APIs.

Why it matters. If you ship a classifier or router in several languages, re-fit thresholds and re-check model choice per language. English results don't transfer, and a task-specific fine-tuned encoder is still a strong baseline.

3. Distilling Literary Sentiment from LLM Annotations into an Ordinal BERT Model

Artem Suslov (Hokkai-Gakuen University), Takehiro Hashimoto (Chuo University) · arXiv, 2 Nov 2026 · arXiv:2611.02985 · models

What they did. Gemma 4 31B labeled 10,889 literary sentences on a 10-point sentiment scale. Five BERT models (Large, Base, Medium, Small, Mini) were trained on those labels with ordinal regression (8,711 train, 1,089 validation, 1,089 test).

Evaluation. 1,923 sentences from Hemingway's The Old Man and the Sea, scored by two human annotators. Compared with the teacher, two general-domain RoBERTa sentiment classifiers and a constant-neutral baseline. MAE, Kendall's tau, and tolerance-based precision and accuracy (±1); separately on 305 non-neutral sentences (scores below 4 or above 6).

Key results. - Rank correlation: teacher 0.604, BERT-large 0.589, BERT-base 0.580. - All five BERT students had lower MAE than the general-domain classifiers. - 84.1% of human scores fell between 4 and 6, so the constant-neutral predictor had the lowest overall MAE (0.597), below every model. - On non-neutral sentences, BERT-base beat the teacher: MAE 0.726 vs 1.116, accuracy (±1) 0.803 vs 0.600. The constant-neutral baseline scored MAE 1.962 and 0 accuracy there.

Caveats. One literary work, no comparison against nominal three-class training, and no variation across random seeds, so the contribution of ordinal modeling is not isolated.

Why it matters. A small encoder distilled from LLM labels can match or beat its teacher where it counts. On skewed label distributions, report metrics on the classes you care about; the overall MAE crowned a model that always says neutral.

4. Finding Errors Is Not Enough: Selective Model Escalation for Entity-Targeted Sentiment Analysis

Nishan Chatterjee, Matthew Purver, Antoine Doucet, Senja Pollak (Jožef Stefan Institute; Queen Mary University of London; University of La Rochelle; University of Ljubljana) · arXiv, 2 Nov 2026 · arXiv:2611.02989

What they did. Entity-level sentiment analysis on long South Slavic news (AspectBench) plus two external datasets (SentiCoref, Slovenian; NewsMTSC, English). After training a primary encoder, they ask which uncertainty signals find errors, whether a second encoder or a local LLM (Gemma 3 27B, 4-bit, prompts optimised with DSPy) improves the selected predictions, and whether development data can pick the policy before test labels are seen.

Method. 80 candidate policies (signal, share routed, action) chosen on a discovery split with a confirmation veto, then frozen and tested. Paired source-group bootstrap intervals (10,000 resamples) and an accounting of extra GPU-minutes.

Key results. - On SentiCoref, the least confident 10% (216 predictions) held 90 of the encoder's errors. A perfect fixer would add 9.14 macro-F1 points; the best evaluated action adds only 0.90. - Replacing with a second encoder corrected 24 of those errors but damaged 59 correct predictions; averaging corrected 20 and harmed 42. - Encoder averaging helps on NewsMTSC (+1.51) but hurts SentiCoref. - Development-selected policies route 30% of records to encoder-assisted Gemma: +1.02 on SentiCoref (interval includes zero) and +1.71 on NewsMTSC. - Sending more records is not consistently better: going from 10% to 20% helped only 7 of 18 dataset-prompt curves. - Chronological data collection is risky: at 20% of the data, HBS reaches 49.46 macro-F1 vs 75.42 for random order.

Caveats. One second-encoder type per external dataset, one LLM, and cost estimates partly from historical timings and assumed prices.

Why it matters. Confidence tells you where to look, not who can fix it. Evaluate a fallback model on the exact predictions it would touch, counting the correct answers it breaks.

5. Saidzoda-Tajik-E5-Large: Fine-Tuning a 560M Embedder for Tajik Retrieval

Tohir Saidzoda, Nekruz Maxudov, Bedil Karimov, Abubakr Abdullozoda, Amin Jalilov, Jonibek Kishvarov, Rahmonali Sharipov, Mariyamkhon Shodyeva (Saidzoda AI Lab, Dushanbe) · arXiv, 31 Oct 2026 · arXiv:2611.00592

What they did. Fine-tuned multilingual-e5-large-instruct (560M) for Tajik question-to-passage retrieval, with the full recipe published.

Method. 235,140 pairs from questions written by their own Tajik LLM for passages from laws, tax documents, Wikipedia and web text; 36% of pairs are English and Russian replay so the model keeps those languages. Hard negatives mined with another embedder and filtered with a positive-aware margin (71% of in-domain pairs got one). Cached multiple-negatives loss, global batch 512, one epoch (466 steps, about 56 minutes on one H100), fp32 with TF32.

Key results. - Recall@10 on held-out laws 80.2 → 93.6%; tax 82.6 → 94.5%. - Belebele Tajik (human-written, the only test not from their generator): 92.2 → 95.2%, intervals touching, so not confirmed. - Russian and English unchanged (97.9 → 98.6, 98.9 → 98.9). - Tied with Giga-Embeddings-3B (five times larger) on in-style law and tax questions; Qwen3-Embedding-8B reaches 76.3 on law. - The same recipe on BGE-M3: +14.0 law, +13.8 tax, +8.4 Belebele Tajik. - What failed: bfloat16 training (loss dropped to exactly zero at step 8), replacing Tajik letters with Russian ones, and merging trained weights back with the originals.

Caveats. Test questions for law, tax and Wikipedia come from the same generator as the training data, so the authors treat those scores as an upper limit. No run without replay.

Why it matters. For a low-resource language, an hour of contrastive fine-tuning on synthetic pairs gets a mid-size embedder level with models several times larger, as long as you keep replay data and check on a human-written set.

Patterns this week

Read the task first. Whether the words give the label away decides between a cheap encoder and a prompt (Riquet). Thresholds and model picks are per language. English-fitted policies let through more errors elsewhere (XDecideBench). Pick metrics that see the classes you care about. A constant neutral can win overall MAE (literary sentiment). Judge a fallback on what it touches. Second opinions break correct answers as well as fix wrong ones (selective escalation).

Hi, and welcome to Small Models, Real Systems, the weekly research digest by Mylari. This is edition six, for Wednesday the fourth of November, 2026. This week: read the task before you pick a model, check that your confidence threshold travels, and know what a second opinion will actually fix.

First, a careful weekend benchmark from Damien Riquet, a lead research engineer at Hornetsecurity, posted on Sunday. On one laptop he compared a bag-of-words baseline, a fine-tuned one hundred and fifty million parameter encoder called Ettin, and a prompted four billion parameter Qwen. On intent classification with a hundred and fifty classes, the encoder scored ninety-three macro F one and the plain prompt under eighty. The encoder answered in under eight milliseconds with one and a half gigabytes of memory. The prompted Qwen took over a second and about eighteen gigabytes. But the lesson is about the task. When the words give the label away, twenty-five examples per intent beat the prompt. When the answer sits in how a comment relates to its topic, it took about a thousand. On sentences written to mislead, all thirteen thousand labels only bought a tie.

Second, XDecideBench, from Marek Šuppa and Jaroslav Kopčan, posted on the first. It tests models that return a probability for each declared option, the kind used to route and moderate text, across a hundred and twenty-two languages. Three open models fine-tuned for these decisions beat the Qwen models they came from in all twelve development languages. But the model that was best in English lost significantly in fifty-six of two hundred and thirty-two suite and language cells. A confidence threshold set on English to allow five percent errors let through eight to twelve percent on German and French. And a fine-tuned mmBERT encoder, trained on the target tasks, scored more than double a zero-shot model built on the same encoder.

Third, Artem Suslov and Takehiro Hashimoto, from the second. They had Gemma four, a thirty-one billion parameter model, score about eleven thousand literary sentences on a ten-point sentiment scale, then trained five BERT models on those labels. Tested against human scores on The Old Man and the Sea, BERT-large nearly matched the teacher's rank correlation, point five nine against point six oh. On sentences that actually carry sentiment, BERT-base beat its teacher, with a mean error of point seven three against one point one two. The trap: eighty-four percent of human scores sat near neutral, so a model that always answers neutral had the lowest overall error of all.

Fourth, Nishan Chatterjee and colleagues at the Jožef Stefan Institute, also from the second, on entity-level sentiment in long South Slavic news. Once an encoder is trained, should its least confident predictions go to a second model? On one Slovenian dataset, the least confident ten percent held ninety errors in two hundred and sixteen predictions. A perfect fixer would add nine points of macro F one. A second encoder fixed twenty-four of those errors but broke fifty-nine correct answers. The best real action gained under one point. Policies picked on development data sent thirty percent of records to a local Gemma model, for gains of one to one point seven points. Uncertainty tells you where to look, not who can fix it.
