Hi, and welcome to Small Models, Real Systems, the weekly research digest by Mylari. This is edition six, for Wednesday the fourth of November, 2026. This week: read the task before you pick a model, check that your confidence threshold travels, and know what a second opinion will actually fix.

First, a careful weekend benchmark from Damien Riquet, a lead research engineer at Hornetsecurity, posted on Sunday. On one laptop he compared a bag-of-words baseline, a fine-tuned one hundred and fifty million parameter encoder called Ettin, and a prompted four billion parameter Qwen. On intent classification with a hundred and fifty classes, the encoder scored ninety-three macro F one and the plain prompt under eighty. The encoder answered in under eight milliseconds with one and a half gigabytes of memory. The prompted Qwen took over a second and about eighteen gigabytes. But the lesson is about the task. When the words give the label away, twenty-five examples per intent beat the prompt. When the answer sits in how a comment relates to its topic, it took about a thousand. On sentences written to mislead, all thirteen thousand labels only bought a tie.

Second, XDecideBench, from Marek Šuppa and Jaroslav Kopčan, posted on the first. It tests models that return a probability for each declared option, the kind used to route and moderate text, across a hundred and twenty-two languages. Three open models fine-tuned for these decisions beat the Qwen models they came from in all twelve development languages. But the model that was best in English lost significantly in fifty-six of two hundred and thirty-two suite and language cells. A confidence threshold set on English to allow five percent errors let through eight to twelve percent on German and French. And a fine-tuned mmBERT encoder, trained on the target tasks, scored more than double a zero-shot model built on the same encoder.

Third, Artem Suslov and Takehiro Hashimoto, from the second. They had Gemma four, a thirty-one billion parameter model, score about eleven thousand literary sentences on a ten-point sentiment scale, then trained five BERT models on those labels. Tested against human scores on The Old Man and the Sea, BERT-large nearly matched the teacher's rank correlation, point five nine against point six oh. On sentences that actually carry sentiment, BERT-base beat its teacher, with a mean error of point seven three against one point one two. The trap: eighty-four percent of human scores sat near neutral, so a model that always answers neutral had the lowest overall error of all.

Fourth, Nishan Chatterjee and colleagues at the Jožef Stefan Institute, also from the second, on entity-level sentiment in long South Slavic news. Once an encoder is trained, should its least confident predictions go to a second model? On one Slovenian dataset, the least confident ten percent held ninety errors in two hundred and sixteen predictions. A perfect fixer would add nine points of macro F one. A second encoder fixed twenty-four of those errors but broke fifty-nine correct answers. The best real action gained under one point. Policies picked on development data sent thirty percent of records to a local Gemma model, for gains of one to one point seven points. Uncertainty tells you where to look, not who can fix it.

Fifth, a retrieval embedder for Tajik from a lab in Dushanbe, posted on the thirty-first. They fine-tuned a five hundred and sixty million parameter multilingual E five model on about two hundred and thirty-five thousand question and passage pairs, written by their own Tajik model, with mined hard negatives and English and Russian pairs mixed in. One epoch took about fifty-six minutes on one graphics card. On unseen laws, the right passage reached the top ten for ninety-three point six percent of questions, up from eighty point two. On the one human-written test, the gain was smaller and not confirmed. And a practical warning: training in bfloat16 collapsed the loss to zero, so they trained in full precision.

The pattern this week: start from the task and the data, not the model. A cheap baseline tells you what kind of problem you have. Check that thresholds and model picks hold in every language you ship. And judge a fallback model on the predictions it would actually touch. Links and numbers are in the show notes.
