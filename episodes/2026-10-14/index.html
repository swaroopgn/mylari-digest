Edition #3. Window: roughly 7 to 14 October 2026; two items (eBay and Amazon, both submitted 6 Oct) are a day older and labelled as such. Every number below comes from the linked source.

This week's theme: the label contract matters as much as the model. Preply replaces a prompted frontier annotator with a 0.8B fine-tune once its teacher data is cleaned and its output is made checkable; eBay finds that the right student (encoder or small generator) depends on the text and on how fast the taxonomy grows; Consultant gets its biggest win by splitting extraction from decision before distilling to 350M; Amazon shows that serving settings alone can change a classifier's predictions; and Enterpret shows generated taxonomies that pass every node-level check and still fail as trees. Five items made the cut.

1. Grammar Concept Annotation at Scale: Deployed Fine-Tuned Small Language Models Outperform Prompted Frontier Models

Marjan Celikik, Ana Peleteiro Ramallo, Javier Morales (Preply) · arXiv, 7 Oct 2026 (v2 11 Oct) · arXiv:2610.10827

What they did. A grammar-mastery tracker for every English learner on the platform. For each learner utterance (plus an upstream corrected sentence) the model emits, in one pass, every curricular grammar concept used (34 in this version), the exact evidence span, a correct/incorrect judgment and a short explanation for errors, as pipe-delimited lines. The product pilot prompted GPT-5.4 with a 3,810-token prompt, which was far over the serving budget at full scale.

Method. Completion-only LoRA on Qwen3.5 (deployed: 0.8B; 4B as a reference comparator). Training data (~187k utterance groups) comes from a curation pipeline: GPT-5.4 fully re-reviews pilot annotations using spaCy-derived linguistic anchors and edit differences, a deterministic validator rejects non-substring spans and invalid labels, and synthetic examples are generated from "structural backbones" (concept and correctness sequences) sampled to boost rare concepts and planted errors. Fine-tuning lets the 0.8B model use a compact 375-token prompt (−90.2%).

Evaluation. Two human-adjudicated benchmarks: Golden (689 utterances, production distribution) and Golden2 (2,977 utterances, error-enriched). Nested criteria: concept (L1), plus exact span (L2), plus correctness (L3); paired bootstrap CIs and randomization tests; raw-supervision controls at matched budgets.

Key results. - Golden, L3 precision / recall: prompted GPT-5.4 67.81 / 62.87, GPT-5.6 Sol 74.37 / 76.04, fine-tuned 0.8B 82.74 / 85.16. Untuned 0.8B was unusable (75.3 to 92.0% parse failures). - The data pipeline vs raw teacher labels raised L3 recall by 36.9 to 40.7 points at matched size, and Rare-9 recall on Golden2 by about 45 points. - An LLM judge rated GPT-5.4's explanations higher (99.06% vs 93.88% for 0.8B), but after human audit all three systems were similar (about 95%): the judge favored its sibling. - Served with vLLM on autoscaled single-GPU g6.2xlarge instances, failures fall back to GPT-5.4; recurring cost is 6.2% of the GPT-5.4 baseline (~16× cheaper); p95 latency 4.3 s. - 35-day A/B test of the whole feature: engagement with progress +15.80%, scheduled hours +2.06%, GMV from new lessons +13.20%.

Why it matters. A full recipe for retiring a frontier annotator: clean the teacher's labels before distilling, make spans checkable by string identity, let fine-tuning absorb the long prompt, and keep the frontier model as a fallback. Also a clean example of why LLM judges need human calibration.

2. Multi-Label Topic Assignment via LLM Distillation: A Comparative Analysis of Generative vs. Discriminative Student Models

Sourabh Kasliwal, Shubhranshu Singh (eBay) · arXiv, 6 Oct 2026 (one day before the window) · arXiv:2610.09063

What they did. GPT-4o labels user-generated content against a taxonomy (iterative prompt tuning with expert audits), and students are trained on those labels for two production uses: buyer-seller conversation topics (112 topics; ~25k training conversations) and category-specific product-review topics (e.g. tread wear for tires; ~39k reviews).

Method. Compare discriminative encoders (DeBERTa-V3, ModernBERT; sigmoid heads, BCE) with LoRA-tuned generative SLMs at 1B, 4B and 8B that write the label list. Conversation examples use progressive context (each turn classified given the earlier turns); 10k extra conversations rich in rare topics were mined by embedding similarity.

Evaluation. Macro-F1 against the distilled oracle labels (so this measures fidelity to GPT-4o), by data type, per-label support, label-set size and latency.

Key results. - Reviews: DeBERTa-V3 0.7047 vs SLM-1B 0.6502, SLM-8B 0.8031. Conversations: DeBERTa-V3 0.4062 vs SLM-1B 0.4501, SLM-8B 0.5404. - Growing the conversation label set from 10 to 112 topics: DeBERTa-V3 0.5435 → 0.3504 (−35.5%), SLM-8B 0.4739 → 0.4728. At 10 labels the encoder was ahead. - Latency: DeBERTa-V3 58 ms, ModernBERT 79 ms, SLM-1B 163 ms, SLM-8B 241 ms. - Generators produced out-of-taxonomy labels (e.g. "shipping-issue") on 0.11 to 0.26% of samples; production maps them to the nearest valid label or drops them. - SLM-8B deployed for both uses (conversations: ~250 requests/s on 9 H100s, P99 under 500 ms). In a 20% ramp A/B test, reviews meeting the quality threshold for approval rose 60%.

Why it matters. A useful decision rule for the student: encoders for short, single-author text with a stable label set; small generators for noisy multi-turn text and taxonomies that keep growing, with a normalization step to enforce the schema.

3. Distilling a job title model to 350M parameters for CPU inference

Consultant (engineering blog) · 10 Oct 2026 · consultant.dev

What they did. Normalize messy multilingual contract-assignment titles ("Systemutvecklare nivå 3, ref 4411") into one clean English job title, on hardware they already rent.

Method. Output contract first: the model returns JSON with role, a verbatim evidence quote and a verbatim level_quote; it never assigns seniority. Deterministic rules check the quotes exist, read the level word, reject company names, IDs and URLs, and retry once on invalid output. Gemma 4 E2B was LoRA-tuned on 2,083 labelled postings and then labelled 19,990 postings; LFM2.5-350M was trained on those plus the originals (LoRA rank 64, 1 epoch).

Evaluation. 200 postings from sources held out by hash (provider-disjoint), answers written under a fixed rubric before any model ran, Wilson 95% intervals.

Key results. - Role matches (of 197): distilled 350M 174 (88.3%), fine-tuned 2B teacher 173 (87.8%), 350M trained on 2,083 rows only 163 (82.7%). Hard failures 25 / 27 / 38 of 200. - On earlier holdouts, switching a general 9B model from writing whole titles to the split output took role matches from 57% to 85% and seniority errors from 65 to 4 of 200. - On a 4-vCPU Hetzner CX33 (EUR 5.49/month) with llama.cpp and a Q8_0 GGUF: about 1,700 postings/hour (~1.2M/month), under EUR 0.0044 per 1,000 titles. The 1B and 1.2B models were slower and no better; a mixture-of-experts model was slow because the task is mostly prompt reading. - Caveats stated plainly: labels were written by an AI reviewer, one run on 200 items, overlapping intervals; the student copies its teacher's mistakes.

Why it matters. Two transferable lessons: split extraction (quote it) from decision (rules decide it) to kill a whole class of hallucinations, and when a tiny student lags, give it more teacher labels before giving it more parameters. Model published on Hugging Face (bytelope/consultant-title-lfm350m-distilled).

4. Same Text, Different Prediction: Serving-Context Nondeterminism in Text Classifiers

Santhosh Kumar Kasa, Siva Rajesh Kasa, Sumit Negi (Amazon) · arXiv, 6 Oct 2026 (one day before the window); NeurIPS 2026 workshops · arXiv:2610.09111

What they did. Measured whether serving settings, not the model or the input, change text-classifier outputs: 180 classifiers (discriminative, pseudo-generative and fully generative; three sizes; five public datasets; three seeds), each evaluated across 3,960 serving contexts (padded length, batch size and composition, precision, quantization, device, backend).

Key results. - Identical contexts reproduce logits bitwise, so every change is caused by the setting varied. - Changing padded length alone changes about 10% of predicted labels, more than any other factor: with learned absolute positions and left padding, every real token moves to a new position. Assigning positions by order among real tokens removes the effect without hurting accuracy. - Batch shape changes no labels in fp32 comparisons, but in bf16 it moves up to 56.7 percentage points of probability mass; label changes concentrate at small margins. - Fully generative classifiers change more labels than discriminative ones under the same serving changes.

Why it matters. Aggregate accuracy and F1 can't see which examples flip, while thresholds, ranking and filters consume the scores. Their recommendation: fix the serving settings, monitor class probabilities rather than labels, and recompute inputs on which fixed settings disagree.

5. From Plausible Hierarchies to Useful Taxonomies: Evaluating Agentic Harnesses on Customer Feedback

Prabhath Chellingi, Raviraja G, Viraj Bagal (Enterpret) · arXiv, 7 Oct 2026 · arXiv:2610.09377

What they did. Six taxonomies over two proprietary customer-feedback corpora (1,940 and 5,000 records): per corpus, a production reference and two runs of the same off-the-shelf agentic construction harness.

Key results. - All six pass every generic LLM-judge check (description completeness 1.00, name-description match 0.97 to 1.00), and a product-coverage check even prefers the generated trees. - Yet 97.7 to 100% of generated leaf names restate an ancestor's name verbatim (references: 13.9% and 2.9%); in one tree all 158 parents at one level have exactly one child. - Routing real records through the trees, a third to three quarters land under more than one top-level product area (references: about a fifth); trees rated equally correct differ by 27 points in cross-branch leakage, and one does worse than a random partition. - Proposed fix: deterministic, judge-free whole-tree metrics (structural discriminators and team partitionability).

Why it matters. If small models tag content against a taxonomy, the taxonomy is part of the system. Node-by-node judging can't catch templated labels or leaky branches; measure the whole tree and the records routed through it.

Patterns this week

Clean, checkable labels beat bigger students. Preply's curation pipeline was worth about 40 points of strict recall; Consultant's 350M student caught its teacher with ten times more teacher labels, not more parameters. Make outputs verifiable: exact substring spans (Preply), verbatim quotes plus rules (Consultant), normalization back to the taxonomy (eBay). Pick the student by the data: encoders for short, stable label sets; small generators for noisy dialogue and growing taxonomies. And the system around the model counts: serving settings can flip labels and move scores (Amazon), and a plausible taxonomy can quietly break every count built on it (Enterpret).

Hi, and welcome to Small Models, Real Systems, the weekly research digest by Mylari. This is edition three, for Wednesday the fourteenth of October, 2026. This week: five picks on what it takes to ship a small model, from the labels it learns from to serving settings nobody looks at. Two of them replace a frontier model outright.

First, Preply, the online tutoring platform. In a paper posted on the seventh of October, they track which grammar concepts English learners use correctly in lesson transcripts. The pilot prompted GPT-5.4 with an almost four-thousand-token prompt, too expensive to roll out. So they fine-tuned a Qwen 3.5 model of just zero point eight billion parameters with LoRA. The training data was teacher annotations that GPT-5.4 re-reviewed and rules validated, plus synthetic examples for rare concepts and errors. That cleanup alone added about forty points of strict recall over raw teacher labels, and the prompt shrank by ninety percent. On human-curated benchmarks the small model beat the prompted frontier models, at about one sixteenth of the serving cost.

Second, eBay. Once GPT-4o has labelled your data, which student should you train: an encoder classifier like DeBERTa, or a small generative model that writes the labels out? It depends on the text. On product reviews, DeBERTa beat a one-billion-parameter generator. On multi-turn buyer-seller chats, even the one-billion model won. And as the chat taxonomy grew from ten topics to a hundred and twelve, DeBERTa's macro F1 fell by about a third, while the eight-billion model barely moved. The catch: generators invent labels like shipping-issue, so production maps them back to the taxonomy or drops them. The eight-billion model now serves both uses, and reviews passing the quality bar rose sixty percent in a live test.

Third, an engineering post from Consultant, a contractor job board, published on the tenth. They turn messy multilingual job titles into one clean English title. The biggest win came before training: the model names the role and quotes its evidence, but never picks the seniority. Rules read the level from a verbatim quote, which stopped invented seniority. Then a fine-tuned two-billion Gemma model labelled about twenty thousand postings for a three-hundred-and-fifty-million-parameter student. On two hundred held-out postings from unseen sources, the student matched its teacher at eighty-eight percent. Trained on the original two thousand rows alone, it trailed. More data mattered, not more parameters. It runs on a five-and-a-half-euro-a-month CPU server.

Fourth, a warning for anyone serving classifiers, from Amazon researchers. They trained a hundred and eighty classifiers, then changed only serving settings, with the same checkpoint and the same text. Changing the padded length alone flipped about ten percent of labels, because tokens shifted to new positions. In full precision, changing batch shape flipped no labels. In bfloat16, it moved up to fifty-seven points of probability mass, which thresholds and rankings consume. Generative classifiers flipped more labels than encoders. Their advice: pin the serving settings, and monitor probabilities, not just labels.

Fifth, taxonomy quality, from Enterpret, a customer-feedback analytics company. They had an agentic harness generate category trees over two real feedback corpora. Every generated tree passed the usual LLM-judge checks on names, parents and siblings. Yet at least ninety-seven percent of their leaf names just restated an ancestor's name, and in one tree, three of every four records fell under more than one top-level category. Their fix is whole-tree metrics: is the shape learned from the data, and do branches split feedback into groups a team can own. If your small model tags against a taxonomy, test the tree too.

The pattern this week: the label contract matters as much as the model. Preply and Consultant won with cleaner, checkable outputs. eBay's best student depended on the text.
